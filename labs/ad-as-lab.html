Some prices move much faster. Airline tickets can change every hour and gasoline every two to three weeks. Some move much slower. Haircuts and newspapers often go more than two years.

Why not change prices right away? Customers like prices they can count on. A business that cuts its price may start a price war with its rivals. And many prices are fixed for a while by menus, catalogs, and contracts.

Key terms

Real GDP
The total amount of goods and services the economy produces, adjusted for inflation. On this graph, 100 is where the economy starts.
Price level
An average of all prices in the economy. When it rises, that is inflation.
Aggregate demand (AD)
Total spending in the economy at each price level: consumption + investment + government spending + net exports. It slopes down.
Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)
How much businesses produce at each price level while wages and other costs are slow to change. It slopes up.
Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) and potential GDP
Potential GDP is what the economy can produce when its workers and machines are used at a normal rate. The LRAS line is vertical at potential GDP.
Recessionary gap
Real GDP is below potential. Businesses need fewer workers, so unemployment is above its normal rate.
Inflationary gap
Real GDP is above potential. Unemployment is below normal, and wages and prices are under pressure to rise.
Natural rate of unemployment
The normal unemployment rate when real GDP equals potential GDP. In this lab it is 4.5%.
Fiscal policy
Changes in government spending and taxes, decided by Congress and the President.
Monetary policy
The Federal Reserve (the Fed) raising or lowering interest rates to change borrowing and spending.
Expansionary and contractionary policy
Expansionary policy boosts spending and shifts AD right. Contractionary policy reduces spending and shifts AD left.
Stagflation
Falling output and rising prices at the same time. It happens when SRAS shifts left.
Sticky prices
Prices that change only now and then, even when demand changes. Menus are printed, contracts are signed, and businesses do not want to be the first to raise prices.
Immediate short run
A period so short that businesses have not changed their prices at all. The aggregate supply curve is flat, so a change in spending changes output only.
Short run
A period long enough for businesses to change the prices they charge, but not long enough for wages and other input costs to change. The aggregate supply curve (SRAS) slopes up.
Flexible prices
Prices that change right away when demand or supply changes. If every price and wage were flexible, a change in spending would change prices only, not output.
Long run
A period long enough for wages and other input costs to change too. Real GDP returns to potential GDP, which is why the long-run aggregate supply curve (LRAS) is vertical.
Self-correction
The economy's return to potential GDP on its own, as wages and other input costs adjust and SRAS shifts. It can be slow, especially when wages need to fall.
Demand-pull and cost-push inflation
Demand-pull inflation starts with a rise in spending: AD shifts right. Cost-push inflation starts with a rise in production costs: SRAS shifts left.
Economic growth
An increase in potential GDP, from better technology, more workers, or more machines. LRAS shifts right.
